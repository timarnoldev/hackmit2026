So why DNA at all, before we get to the problems with it?

Density: the often-cited figure is around 215 petabytes per gram, theoretically. Nothing else comes close.

Durability: stored cold, dry and dark, DNA's decay rate points to centuries of stability, no power needed. Compare that to hard drives and tape, which need refreshing every 5 to 10 years or you risk losing the data.

That combination matters for cold archives, long-horizon scientific and genomic records, compliance data with decade-plus retention requirements, and disaster-resilient backups where a tiny physical footprint is worth a lot.

So every DNA storage pipeline follows a few hand-written rules. Never more than three of the same letter in a row. Keep GC content between 40 and 60%. Add 30% extra strands, just in case.

They were chosen once, copied from paper to paper. And they go onto every machine. Nanopore adds and drops letters, especially in runs. Illumina mostly swaps single letters.

Same rules on Nanopore. Same rules on Illumina.

One real blind spot

One real pattern, learned on both sides

What the channel actually does

On real Nanopore reads, the base right after a CCCT run is deleted far more often than elsewhere — measured directly from the 5-mer context table fit on real train reads, and one of the leading hot-spot families in two independent datasets (Microsoft and DNAformer).

GACAGTCCCTAGCATG deletions land right after this run

The hand rule doesn't catch it: "no run longer than 3" allows CCC outright, since it's exactly the limit. No hand-written rule ever looks at this pattern.

Two learned models, the same discovery

The risk model steers the encoder around it. Where an equally valid alternate seed exists, it scores a candidate that ends a slot in CCCT as riskier, and the encoder keeps the other one instead — for free, since trying another seed costs no density.

discarded…GACCCTGTC…
kept…GATGACAGTC…same data, different seed

The polisher learned the same thing from the decoding side. Its roughly 65-position receptive field lets it down-weight the base right after CCCT even when the majority vote disagrees, because training on real reads taught it that position fails more than most.

Neither model was told about CCCT. One found it from decoder failures, the other from correcting reads — independently, from a real channel property no fixed rulebook could have written down in advance.

This is where the previous slide's complaint stops being abstract.

On real Nanopore reads, the base right after a CCCT run gets deleted more than elsewhere. It's measured, it shows up in two independent datasets, and the hand rule never catches it because CCC is exactly the legal limit of 3.

That's the strongest version of the tier two claim: not "the model found some pattern", but this specific, checkable, real one, confirmed twice.

In one diagram

What we actually built

Zoom out for a second: here is the whole system in one diagram, before we walk through the pieces.

The storage path. A channel profile describes one situation as JSON: error rates, the 5-letter context table, dropout, the read budget, costs. It is fit on real reads. The encoder is a Fountain code: for every slot it makes 8 to 32 candidate strands, puts a checksum in each, and a scorer keeps one. Simulator A plays the lab: dropouts, uneven coverage, per-read quality, errors every read shares, and context errors. The decoder chain gets the noisy reads back. Then a checksum turns a wrong strand into a missing one, and the Fountain code rebuilds the file.

The one learned part in that path is the polisher. The classic baseline aligns the reads and votes a draft; a small dilated CNN, about 0.8 million parameters, looks at 17 vote features per position and says keep, substitute, delete, or insert.

Around it runs the loop. Freeze the decoder, label strands by simulating each one 32 times to see where the decoder fails, train the risk model on those labels, search the settings, and hand the risk model back to the encoder so it keeps the safest candidate. At most three rounds.

Everything is judged by one evaluation: 300 held-out trials, the fewest reads per strand at the recovery target, written to a result file that the dashboard, this deck and the demo all read. And Simulator B sits outside the loop, built from different mechanisms, only to check that a gain survives.

Indigo is learned, grey is classic code. Two learned models in the whole system. The rest of the deck walks through these pieces one at a time.

Appendix A3

Questions we expect

You have no wet lab. How do you know it's real?

We don't synthesize or sequence anything. The simulator matches real held-out reads within about 3 points at every read count. Every gain must survive Simulator B, and the risk model's ranking is checked on real reads:

Isn't this just DNA Fountain?

The encoder is DNA Fountain style on purpose. We change how it's configured: which rules, how much redundancy, which candidate to keep, per channel. We don't claim a better encoder.

What if the hand rules are already optimal?

Then the tool says so, with measurements, and that's useful. The audit is a measurement, not a bet against the rules.

Isn't the improvement just your learned decoder?

No. The default, system B, already uses the same decoder chain. B to C isolates the rule audit, C to D learned selection. A to B is the polisher, and we report that separately. Both tiers also work with plain majority vote.

Answer in one or two sentences, then stop.

Appendix A4

Questions we expect

Couldn't you buy accuracy with more redundancy?

That's what we rule out. Codecs are judged on the Pareto front of bits per base against reads per strand, at a fixed recovery target.

How is this different from DNAformer or adaptive constrained coding?

Those optimize the decoder, the constraints or the redundancy on their own. We close the loop: the decoder's failures on a channel decide what the encoder avoids, measured against a fixed target.

Why not learn the encoder end to end?

Insertions and deletions are hard to differentiate through, and storage needs every bit back. The Fountain code guarantees exact recovery; learning only picks which candidates to use.

What has the risk model actually learned?

The first version rediscovered the run-length rule, and its real-read ranking was weak. So we measured real context errors and put them into the simulator.

Answer in one or two sentences, then stop.

Appendix A5

Questions we expect

The Microsoft dataset has known issues. Does that matter?

Yes, and we handle it. Its references aren't uniformly random, so we use it for calibration and benchmarks, never to learn risky motifs. Simulator B uses a context table fit on DNAformer.

Does the polisher beat the baseline?

On real held-out clusters, exact strands go from 41.0% to 56.8% at 4 reads, 67.0% to 82.5% at 6 reads and 90.0% to 95.1% at 16 reads. The room is at low coverage, where reading is cheap. Both claim tiers compare codecs with the same decoder, so they hold either way.

What would a real user do with this?

Describe their channel as a profile, calibrate it on a sample of their own reads, and let the tool say which rules and how much redundancy to use. Cost figures use placeholder prices.

What did the GX10 do?

Decoder training, risk labels (each strand simulated 32 times and decoded), and 300 held-out recovery trials per final codec, spread across its 20 cores. See A3.
