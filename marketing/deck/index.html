So why DNA at all, before we get to the problems with it?

Density: the often-cited figure is around 215 petabytes per gram, theoretically. Nothing else comes close.

Durability: stored cold, dry and dark, DNA's decay rate points to centuries of stability, no power needed. Compare that to hard drives and tape, which need refreshing every 5 to 10 years or you risk losing the data.

That combination matters for cold archives, long-horizon scientific and genomic records, compliance data with decade-plus retention requirements, and disaster-resilient backups where a tiny physical footprint is worth a lot.

So every DNA storage pipeline follows a few hand-written rules. Never more than three of the same letter in a row. Keep GC content between 40 and 60%. Add 30% extra strands, just in case.

They were chosen once, copied from paper to paper. And they go onto every machine. Nanopore adds and drops letters, especially in runs. Illumina mostly swaps single letters.

Same rules on Nanopore. Same rules on Illumina.

Each rule costs you storage or reads. Nobody measures whether they actually pay off.

Where the rules come in

So where do the rules come in?

For every slot, the encoder generates several candidate strands. Same data, different seeds.

The hard rules throw some out: this one has a run of five, this one is too low in GC.

By default, any candidate that passes is as good as any other. But watch this one: it passes every hand rule, and it's still carrying a real deletion hot spot from real Nanopore reads.

That's exactly what the risk model is for. It scores every survivor, catches the one the hand rules missed, and keeps the one that's actually safest.

In one diagram

What we actually built

Zoom out for a second: here is the whole system in one diagram, before we walk through the pieces.

The storage path. A channel profile describes one situation as JSON: error rates, the 5-letter context table, dropout, the read budget, costs. It is fit on real reads. The encoder is a Fountain code: for every slot it makes 8 to 32 candidate strands, puts a checksum in each, and a scorer keeps one. Simulator A plays the lab: dropouts, uneven coverage, per-read quality, errors every read shares, and context errors. The decoder chain gets the noisy reads back. Then a checksum turns a wrong strand into a missing one, and the Fountain code rebuilds the file.

The one learned part in that path is the polisher. The classic baseline aligns the reads and votes a draft; a small dilated CNN, about 0.8 million parameters, looks at 17 vote features per position and says keep, substitute, delete, or insert.

Around it runs the loop. Freeze the decoder, label strands by simulating each one 32 times to see where the decoder fails, train the risk model on those labels, search the settings, and hand the risk model back to the encoder so it keeps the safest candidate. At most three rounds.

Everything is judged by one evaluation: 300 held-out trials, the fewest reads per strand at the recovery target, written to a result file that the dashboard, this deck and the demo all read. And Simulator B sits outside the loop, built from different mechanisms, only to check that a gain survives.

Indigo is learned, grey is classic code. Two learned models in the whole system. The rest of the deck walks through these pieces one at a time.

Tier 2 learned selection

Same codec. Same reads. Only the scorer changes.

This is the cleanest evidence in the project, so take it slowly.

Everything is held fixed. Same codec, same redundancy, same strand length, same hard rules, the same 32 candidates per slot, the same decoder, the same seeds, the same file. One thing changes: which model ranks those candidates.

Rank them with the hand rules, and at four and a half reads per strand the file comes back in 27% of 300 trials.

Rank them with the model that learned from decoder failures, and it comes back 98.3% of the time. Seventy-one points, at a standard error of 2.7. And choosing among candidates is free: no extra strands, no extra reads, no density.

We then repeated the whole thing with the learned decoder, which is the harder test because a better decoder repairs more of what the model steers around. Strand failures still fall by 2.8 points, on our simulator and on the firewall simulator.

If asked where this comes from: 300 held-out trials per point, run 2 strict, raw curve in the repo. The ablation ladder's C to D rung is a worse measurement of the same thing, because the settings search can move redundancy between rungs; we quote this paired experiment instead. One blemish we keep visible: at five reads the learned codec sits at 0.997, one failed trial in 300, where the hand rules reach 1.000.

Where it runs

The whole codec fits on a microcontroller

Both learned models run on a microcontroller. The polisher quantized to int8, the risk model in float32. No laptop, no network.

So if we are behind on reconstruction, what is new here?

Every published method in that comparison answers one question: given noisy reads, recover the strand.

None of them answers this one: on your channel, does this coding rule pay for what it costs? You cannot answer it without a decoder in the loop, which is what our loop is.

And the answer differs by channel. Run length pays off on Nanopore and buys nothing on Illumina. GC is the other way round. Each standard rule earns its place on exactly one of our two channels, and you only know which by measuring it.

Appendix A3

Questions we expect

You have no wet lab. How do you know it's real?

We don't synthesize or sequence anything. The simulator matches real held-out reads within about 3 points at every read count. Every gain must survive Simulator B, and the risk model's ranking is checked on real reads:

Isn't this just DNA Fountain?

The encoder is DNA Fountain style on purpose. We change how it's configured: which rules, how much redundancy, which candidate to keep, per channel. We don't claim a better encoder.

What if the hand rules are already optimal?

Then the tool says so, with measurements, and that's useful. The audit is a measurement, not a bet against the rules.

Isn't the improvement just your learned decoder?

No. The default, system B, already uses the same decoder chain. B to C isolates the rule audit, C to D learned selection. A to B is the polisher, and we report that separately. Both tiers also work with plain majority vote.

Appendix A4

Questions we expect

Couldn't you buy accuracy with more redundancy?

That's what we rule out. Codecs are judged on the Pareto front of bits per base against reads per strand, at a fixed recovery target.

How is this different from DNAformer or adaptive constrained coding?

Those optimize the decoder, the constraints or the redundancy on their own. We close the loop: the decoder's failures on a channel decide what the encoder avoids, measured against a fixed target.

Why not learn the encoder end to end?

Insertions and deletions are hard to differentiate through, and storage needs every bit back. The Fountain code guarantees exact recovery; learning only picks which candidates to use.

What has the risk model actually learned?

The first version rediscovered the run-length rule, and its real-read ranking was weak. So we measured real context errors and put them into the simulator.

Answer in one or two sentences, then stop.

Appendix A5

Questions we expect

The Microsoft dataset has known issues. Does that matter?

Yes, and we handle it. Its references aren't uniformly random, so we use it for calibration and benchmarks, never to learn risky motifs. Simulator B uses a context table fit on DNAformer.

Does the polisher beat the baseline?

On real held-out clusters, exact strands go from 41.0% to 56.8% at 4 reads, 67.0% to 82.5% at 6 reads and 90.0% to 95.1% at 16 reads. The room is at low coverage, where reading is cheap. Both claim tiers compare codecs with the same decoder, so they hold either way.

What would a real user do with this?

Describe their channel as a profile, calibrate it on a sample of their own reads, and let the tool say which rules and how much redundancy to use. Cost figures use placeholder prices.

What did the GX10 do?

Decoder training, risk labels (each strand simulated 32 times and decoded), and 300 held-out recovery trials per final codec, spread across its 20 cores. See A3.

Appendix A6

What the model learned, and we did not teach it

We never taught the risk model a rule. It only ever saw which strands our decoder got wrong. Afterwards we asked it what it thinks is dangerous.

It found the homopolymer rule by itself. Risk is flat up to a run of 4, then it climbs. The standard rule in the field forbids runs longer than 3, so the model would draw the line one or two letters looser.

We tested that, and the model is wrong about where to draw it. Moving the limit from 3 to 4 drops file recovery from 81 percent to 33 percent at 12 reads. The model answers a per-strand question and the difference there really is small; a file is 1239 strands and needs all of them, so small per-strand differences compound. It is a good guide to what is dangerous and a bad guide to where the line goes, which is exactly why we measure in reads per strand instead of trusting a score.

On GC it is flat, 3.6 points across the whole range. It calls the GC rule pointless, and our rule audit reached the same verdict from a completely different measurement.

The patterns it names are all runs, as expected. The interesting part is the order: G and C runs rank above A and T runs. No hand rule makes that distinction, it just counts identical letters.

And the cross-check. Our channel table, fitted on real reads, says the worst deletion contexts are GAAAG and AAAAG. The model never saw that table and rates them 0.93 and 0.97. The worst substitution context it shrugs at, 0.44, because voting across reads repairs a substitution while a deletion shifts everything after it. It did not learn where errors happen. It learned where errors are fatal. On Illumina every pattern comes back at zero, correctly, because there the decoder does not fail on sequence grounds at all.

About 35 seconds. Source: docs/LEARNED_RULES.md, probed from the trained model of the final run.

Appendix A7

The two models

Two learned models in the whole system, and they sit at opposite ends of it.

The polisher is the decoder's second half. It never sees raw letters: it gets the classic decoder's draft and its vote columns, 17 numbers per position, votes for each letter, for a deletion, for an inserted letter and which one, coverage, the draft base, agreement and where we are in the strand, all as fractions. A dilated 1D CNN, stem of kernel 5 and eight residual blocks with dilations one, two, four, eight, twice over, 128 channels, about 0.8 million parameters and a receptive field of about 65 positions. Two heads: per position keep, substitute or delete, per gap insert or nothing. We apply the edits so deletions and insertions balance, which keeps the strand length exact. It trained in 13 minutes.

The risk model is the opposite. It sees one candidate strand as one-hot letters, nothing else, and returns one number between 0 and 1: the predicted fraction of decoding attempts that fail for that strand. The labels come from simulating each training strand 32 times and decoding it, so the target is a failure rate, not a yes or no. It plugs into the encoder as the scorer that picks among the 8 to 32 candidates for every slot.

And this is the arrow that closes the loop: the decoder's failures become the risk model's labels, so what the decoder gets wrong decides what the encoder writes next time.

About 35 seconds. Details in docs/MODELS.md.
