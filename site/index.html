Measure the rule. Keep what pays.

Every DNA coding rule is a hypothesis. We measure which ones pay off.

Erbgut audits the sequence rules and the redundancy a DNA storage pipeline uses, one sequencing channel at a time, then tunes the codec to what the measurement says. Same encoder, same decoder, one fixed recovery target on both sides of every comparison.

Erbgut is German for the genetic material an organism inherits and passes on. The repository name is Adaptive DNA Codec.

The problem

Writing DNA keeps getting cheaper. Reading it is the bill that comes back.

The medium is real, and the industry is organised

Archival demand keeps growing. IDC forecast in 2018 that the global datasphere would grow from 33 zettabytes in 2018 to 175 zettabytes by 2025, and that more than 22 zettabytes of storage capacity would have to ship across all media types in between (IDC and Seagate, The Digitization of the World, November 2018).

DNA is no longer only an academic idea. The DNA storage industry now runs as a SNIA Technology Affiliate and has published interoperability specifications, Sector Zero and Sector One, which is what a field does when it expects products.

Writing is still the gating cost, and it is the one everyone is attacking. Array synthesis was estimated at about $0.0001 per base in 2019, which the authors put at roughly $800 million per terabyte, seven to eight orders of magnitude above tape (Carlson's estimate, reported in Ceze, Nivala and Strauss, Molecular digital data storage using DNA, Nature Reviews Genetics, 2019). Array synthesis already sits two to four orders of magnitude below column synthesis (Kosuri and Church, Nature Methods, 2014).

Reading is the bill that comes back

Sequencing has fallen further and faster than almost anything in computing. NHGRI's cost accounting puts the price of producing one megabase of sequence at $0.0058 as of its May 2022 data point, against $5,292.39 in September 2001 (NHGRI, DNA Sequencing Costs: Data, figures in the May 2022 data table).

You still pay it every time you read, and you pay it per read, not per strand. The DNA Data Storage Alliance derives a sequencing cost of $800 to $1,500 per gigabyte of stored data, and its own footnote states the assumption behind it: 10x read coverage, that is ten reads of every fragment, so that none is missed (An Introduction to DNA Data Storage, June 2021). The same document notes that sequencing cost is what gates archival use, because archives get read.

Published experiments show the same multiplier in practice. Organick and colleagues recovered files at a mean sequencing coverage of 35x with about ten physical copies of each sequence, and wrote plainly that increasing sequencing coverage significantly increases the cost of recovery (Probing the physical limits of reliable DNA data retrieval, Nature Communications, 2020).

The rules are folklore

To survive that noise, pipelines follow rules that were chosen once, copied between papers, and then applied to every sequencing channel: no run of the same letter longer than three, GC content between 40 and 60%, a fixed amount of redundancy, often 30% extra strands. Nanopore and Illumina fail in very different ways, and the rules do not distinguish between them.

Every rule costs something. A rule that rejects candidate strands costs density or reads. Redundancy costs strands you have to synthesize. If a rule does not reduce failures on your channel, you are paying for it and getting nothing back.

What we sell, mechanically

Notice what the Alliance's dollar figure actually rests on. It is a cost per gigabyte at an assumed coverage. Coverage is the multiplier, and it is set by how many reads your codec and decoder need before the file comes back intact. That quantity, reads per strand at the recovery target, is what Erbgut optimises, at a fixed density, with the decoder held constant so the improvement cannot come from anywhere else.

The same measurement also produces the opposite answer when it is true. If the hand rules are already near optimal on a channel, the audit says so, with numbers instead of assumption.

What we built

Two tiers, one fixed target

Every setting is judged the same way: a fixed 20 KB file has to come back exactly in all 300 held-out trials. Then we count the reads per strand and the bits per base it took. The encoder and the decoder never change between the two sides of a comparison. Only the rules, the redundancy and the choice of candidate strands do.

Tier 1

Rule audit

Switch each rule off, one at a time, on one channel. Measure the reads per strand and bits per base needed to hit the recovery target. Tune the redundancy the same way. Keep only what pays off. This is the tool's main output, and it is a verdict per rule and per channel.

The result we did not expect: the homopolymer rule pays off on Nanopore and does nothing on Illumina. The GC rule does the exact opposite. Both rules are applied to both channels today.

Tier 2

Learned selection

A Fountain encoder can write the same data as many different strands at no cost in density. A small CNN, trained only on where the decoder actually failed on this channel, scores the candidates, and the encoder keeps the safest one.

On Nanopore at six reads per strand this cuts strand failures from 51.96% to 48.01%, a difference of 3.95 points (95% confidence interval 4.18 to 3.72 points), measured on paired held-out trials with the same decoder on both sides. On Illumina the difference is about zero, which is the correct answer: that channel does not fail on sequence grounds.

Read the redundancy row honestly. On Nanopore the tuned codec reaches the target at 5.5 reads per strand instead of 19.5, but it gets there at 0.64 bits per base against the default's 1.202, so it is buying reads with density. That is a real trade and a useful one when sequencing is the recurring cost, but it is not a free lunch, and we do not present it as one. On Illumina the trade runs the other way: same read budget, more data per base.

Evidence

Built so that we cannot fool ourselves

We have no wet lab. Everything is measured on a channel simulator calibrated on real sequencing reads, and checked back against real held-out reads. The evidence design was fixed before any run, and every comparison isolates one change.

Held-out means held out

Every seed used for training, tuning or choosing a setting comes from a separate train pool. The real data split is fixed in code: every fifth cluster, 1,996 of 10,000, never used for training, tuning or calibration. A codec is evaluated on held-out seeds exactly once, after it is chosen.

The recovery target is absolute: the file comes back exactly in all 300 held-out trials, or the setting does not count. Candidate settings are screened at 50 trials, re-checked at 300 on train seeds, and given a margin check at 10% fewer reads.

The ablation isolates the claim

Five systems on the same channel and the same held-out seeds. The default we compare against always uses the same decoder as the tuned codec, so no gain can leak in from the decoder. Step B to C is the rule audit. Step C to D is learned selection.

Run 1's numbers were thrown out and are marked superseded in our own notes: they were a winner's curse from picking the best of hundreds of settings on the same trials, and the final codec then missed the target on held-out data, 297 of 300. Run 2 added a margin check, three seed blocks and train-only selection. We say this openly because it is the reason the later numbers can be trusted.

The sim-to-real firewall

The loop might learn a quirk of our simulator instead of a property of DNA. Two checks catch that.

A structurally different second simulator, built from different mechanisms and never used for optimisation, reproduces the tier 2 gain: strand failures 50.92% down to 46.76%, a difference of 4.16 points (95% CI 4.37 to 3.96).

Real reads. Holding coverage fixed, our risk model ranks real decoder failures at AUC 0.69, against 0.66 for the homopolymer rule, 0.61 for a calibrated 5-mer context table and 0.50, chance, for GC deviation. Holding coverage and longest run fixed, so the hand rule's own feature is neutralised, the model still reaches 0.61 while the rule drops to 0.50. On real DNA the model knows something the hand rule does not.

Why it is defensible

Accuracy per unit of compute, and a question nobody else asks

Same accuracy, 1/125 the model

Our decoder matches a fine-tuned DNAformer at four, six and ten reads on the same dataset (66.1, 88.8 and 96.2% against 65.9, 88.3 and 95.7%) with 0.8M parameters against their 100M, 577k training examples against 1.4 billion, and 13 minutes of training on one machine instead of GPU days.

The reason is placement, not scale. The classic algorithm already does the alignment well. The small model only corrects what is left, using the local context and the coverage that voting throws away. A from-scratch 9.6M-parameter transformer that tried to learn the whole task failed, and that failure is documented in the repository.

It runs where the others cannot

The same decoder fits on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller and reconstructs a strand there in 170 ms, so the demo runs unplugged from any computer. No published trace reconstruction system runs on a chip that cheap. That is what accuracy per unit of compute buys you in practice.

The methodology is the product

Every published system in this space reconstructs strands. None of them measures what a coding rule or a redundancy level costs and buys on a given channel at a fixed recovery target. The audit harness, the fixed objective, the ablation ladder and the sim-to-real firewall are the asset, and they apply to any channel and any codec, not only ours.

A risk model is specific to one channel, one read budget and one decoder. That is the point: the same strand can be safe on one channel and risky on another, and the tool is what tells you which.

Where we are behind, plainly. On raw reconstruction accuracy, TReconLM (TMLR 2025) is better than us at every read count from two to ten on this dataset, 91.2% against our 88.8% at six reads, and it trains on the same split we do. We are level with a fine-tuned DNAformer and ahead of the classical algorithms. The decoder was never meant to be a contribution to trace reconstruction. It had to be solid, fast and identical on both sides of every comparison, and it is. The full table is in the repository, written to be unflattering where the numbers are.

And the limit that matters most. Everything here is simulated on a channel calibrated against real reads, and checked back against real held-out reads. We have shown that the risk model's ranking transfers to real DNA. We have not shown that selecting candidate strands by it reduces failures on real DNA, because that needs new strands synthesized and sequenced. No wet lab work went into this project, and nothing on this page should be read as if it had.

What is next

Three things, in the order they matter

None of these is done. They are listed because they are the honest answer to what would have to be true for this to be a product rather than a measurement.

  1. Wet lab validation

    Synthesize and sequence a file encoded with a tuned codec and one encoded with the default, on the same run, and check whether the reads per strand we predict is the reads per strand we need. This is the one experiment that converts a calibrated simulation into a claim about DNA. Until it is run, every number here carries that caveat.

  2. Inner codes

    Right now a strand tells the decoder only what it reads. An inner code inside each strand would let it also say where it is probably wrong, so the decoder spends its effort on the positions that need it. That is the largest remaining gap to the published leaders at low coverage, where the whole economic argument lives.

  3. Per channel decoders

    Today one decoder is adapted per channel inside the loop. The next step is a decoder trained against the specific codec it will be paired with, so the encoder and the decoder co-adapt across rounds instead of the encoder adapting to a frozen decoder. The alternating structure is already built for it.
